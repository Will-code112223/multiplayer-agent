## Key Question 1: What do judges (2024–2026) say distinguishes winners?

### Takeaway
Judges across JetBrains (June 2026), Devpost, and multiple 2026 dev.to judge write-ups converge on the same ranking: (1) a one-sentence, instantly legible identity; (2) a problem the judge personally feels; (3) a working, narrow, end-to-end live demo shown within ~90 seconds; (4) honest, deliberate scope cuts. Polish beyond readability, framework choice, and team credentials barely move scores.

### Cited Findings
- JetBrains x Codex Hackathon judge write-up (June 2026): "A strong project with a confusing demo loses to a simpler project that the judges understand." — [JetBrains blog, June 2026](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)
- Jono Bacon (CEO, Stateshift), same post: "Say what the problem is that you're solving. You need to get your audience of judges sharing your frustration with the problem that you see in the world." He also suggests being able to show something working within about 90 seconds. — [JetBrains blog, June 2026](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)
- Avi Press (Scarf), same post: be explicit about the problem and how your submission addresses it; put judges in the user's shoes; be direct about what works and what doesn't. — [JetBrains blog, June 2026](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)
- Bonnie Xu (OpenAI), same post: don't start with "what's the coolest new tool I can use?" but with "what's something I can build now that I probably couldn't have built before?"; pick a specific workflow you personally find annoying; she favors one clear "this is now possible" moment over a tour of every feature. The post's thesis: the best projects do one thing really well rather than five things halfway. — [JetBrains blog, June 2026](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)
- Colin Lowenberg (Nebius), same post: mock slow parts and pre-fill data so the demo doesn't stall. The post also advises reading the rules, finding out who the judges are, and using tools/people you already know. — [JetBrains blog, June 2026](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)
- A judge who scored three hackathons in 2026 (post ~late July 2026): a working live demo was "the biggest differentiator"; opening with a one-sentence description of the product helped more than opening with context; a narrow, well-scoped project scored higher than an ambitious one; polish beyond readability, framework choice, and team credentials did not move scores. Quote: "When the panel reconvenes and someone says 'which one was the scheduling one,' the projects that survive that conversation are the ones with a one-line identity." Also: "a team that built a platform in 36 hours reads as a team that did not make good decisions." He notes most teams land mid-range on the named rubric categories and the difference comes from things the rubric doesn't name. — [dev.to, kurbaitaev, ~July 2026](https://dev.to/kurbaitaev/what-judges-actually-score-notes-from-a-year-of-hackathon-judging-3p4l)
- Another judge/participant rubric (dev.to, 2026) ranks four priorities: (1) "The first thirty seconds decide whether the judge cares. Name the person, the situation and what goes wrong for them today." (2) "A working demo of a narrow feature beats a slide deck about a broad platform every time." (3) "Teams that cut scope on purpose and can explain why look like engineers." (4) "A judge will ask one technical question to test whether the person answering understands the system." His weighted rubric: problem clarity 25%, working demo 30%, scope judgement/honesty about limitations 20%, pitch clarity 15%, feasibility beyond the weekend 10%. — [dev.to, pranjulrathour](https://dev.to/pranjulrathour/what-hackathon-judges-actually-look-for-a-rubric-from-both-sides-of-the-table-39o)
- Devpost's own blog lists the common criteria organizers use: technical implementation, ease of use, demo, potential impact, quality of the idea, design; on the Devpost platform criteria are usually weighted equally (1–5 stars each), and changing weights requires offline judging. Devpost also published "How to win a hackathon: Advice from 5 seasoned judges." — [Devpost blog: criteria](https://info.devpost.com/blog/understanding-hackathon-submission-and-judging-criteria); [Devpost blog: 5 judges](https://info.devpost.com/blog/hackathon-judging-tips); [Devpost help: online judging](https://help.devpost.com/hc/en-us/articles/360021798712-How-to-judge-an-online-Hackathon)
- Devpost survey (via third-party summary): 52% of participants think judges should prioritize creativity/innovation; 28% think potential impact. — [Eventflare](https://eventflare.io/journal/crafting-effective-hackathon-judging-criteria-a-step-by-step-guide)
- Reskilll (platform, 2026 guide from "5000+ events"): judges score "your product, your pitch, and your potential rather than your code"; technical implementation ~20–25% (projects that crash, rely on hardcoded data, or are mockups score low); impact/usefulness ~20–25% (needs a clearly defined target user and measurable outcomes); a generic app or chatbot scores poorly; prepare for Q&A on differentiation, business model, and "what would you do with more time." — [Reskilll judging criteria](https://blogs.reskilll.com/?p=436); [Reskilll 2026 guide](https://reskilll.com/blogs/how-to-win-a-hackathon-in-2026-reskillls-guide-from-5000-events/)
- MLH organizer guidance: prefers science-fair-style table judging and stack-ranking to normalize across judges; suggests short demo videos over presentations. — [MLH judging plan](https://guide.mlh.com/general-information/judging-and-submissions/judging-plan)
- hacksu Hackathon-Judging-Guide: judges score up to six categories on 1–5 scales; consistency across projects is stressed; judges needn't know how weights combine. — [hacksu guide (mirror)](https://skwrus.duckdns.org/hacksu/Hackathon-Judging-Guide)
- Repeat winner Ray Deck (Nov 2022, online hackathons): judging buckets like "completeness" and "wow factor" are generic; unwritten incentives matter more; "judges did not necessarily reward superior technical output"; the project must "deliver an experience that solves the problem," not just slides and code. — [Sustained Writing, Nov 2022](https://sustained.substack.com/p/lessons-from-winning-five-hackathons)
- Judge bias: a project can satisfy the published criteria and still miss a given judge's unwritten personal rubric. — [dev.to, sumit0rn](https://dev.to/sumit0rn/when-judge-bias-matters-more-than-eligibility-the-hidden-problem-with-modern-hackathons-1h73)

### Inferences
- For SpeedHacks, the judges are founders (operators), not partners, and there is no published rubric, so the unwritten-rubric findings dominate: lead with the one-liner, make them feel the bottleneck, demo inside 90 seconds, and be candid about scope.
- Because every speedrun portfolio company gives a "Best Use" prize, wiring in one sponsor product (deeply, not superficially) is a second, cheaper shot at a win and is likely noticed by the founder-judges from those companies.

### Gaps
- No a16z-partner-authored statement of hackathon judging criteria was found; the SpeedHacks rubric is unpublished.
- Could not read the Devpost "5 seasoned judges" article body; only its existence is confirmed.

## Key Question 2: What do winners of short hackathons say about time budgeting, scoping, when to stop building, and rehearsal?

### Takeaway
Winners consistently (a) freeze scope within the first ~10–15% of the clock, (b) have one input producing one output end-to-end by ~25% of the clock, (c) stop adding features with 15–25% of the clock left (2 hours before judging in a 12-hour event), and (d) spend that last block on seed data, rehearsal (5–10 run-throughs), and a backup recording. Several say more hackathons are won at the pitch stage than during coding.

### Cited Findings
- 8x winner's 24-hour plan (Medium, ~May 2026): first 2 hours finalize idea, freeze scope, pick stack, sketch user flow, commit to one core feature ("the exact approach used in most winning teams"); next ~4 hours add one "wow factor" (AI component, automation, dashboard), then integration/testing; final 3–4 hours demo script, slides, pitch, video. Claims more hackathons are won at the pitch stage than during coding. (Caveat: the article's bulleted timeline is internally inconsistent; body text is the intended plan.) — [Medium, codetodeploy, ~May 2026](https://medium.com/codetodeploy/the-hackathon-insider-playbook-from-a-8x-winner-de83d138bab5)
- Other playbooks in the same set: a 5-time winner spends five full hours planning before coding in a two-day event; another suggests the first 3 hours for planning/setup and the last 3 hours for presentation practice. — [Medium, eyal.shechtman](https://medium.com/@eyal.shechtman/hack-the-hackathon-a-proven-formula-for-winning-231663ff00cc); [Medium, allankong](https://medium.com/@allankong/ive-won-thousands-in-hackathons-here-are-my-tips-and-strategies-72267f9f3974)
- Masai School 2026 guide: "hackathon outcomes are decided in the first quarter of the clock"; target one input producing one output by ~25% of the time; a rough working skeleton early beats stronger raw skill; favors 2–3 people with a dedicated presenter; pick a problem you can finish; suggests ~30% of pitch time on problem, 70% on solution. — [Masai School, 2026](https://www.masaischool.com/blog/how-to-win-an-ai-hackathon-in-2026-full-guide/)
- Hacker News commenter rule of thumb: pick "a project you can implement in a quarter of the time allotted." Another: practicing the pitch "is the only thing that actually counts in the vast majority of hackathons"; one recalls a team winning with a strong presentation and zero working code; one found videos "eat up presentation time because of switching delays." — [HN: Best Hackathon Practices](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=6419839); [HN: How to win without first place](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=27831250); [HN: Hackathon tips](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=1366795)
- Feature-freeze timing in guides: ~hour 18 of 24 (last 6 hours for bug fixes, UI polish, demo script, README, end-to-end test); "don't start new features in the last four hours of a 24-hour event, and don't refactor"; one event schedules a code-freeze warning 1 hour before hacking ends. — [Reskilll idea-to-app](https://blogs.reskilll.com/from-idea-to-app-a-step-by-step-guide-to-hackathon-success/); [HackerEarth 10 tips](https://www.hackerearth.com/blog/10-tips-win-hackathon)
- Live-demo technical checklist (dev.to, ~early Sept 2026): stop adding features about two hours before judging; "run the demo path ten times, fix what breaks, and write down the exact sequence of clicks"; "the teams that lose points are usually the ones deploying a fix at minute fifty-nine." — [dev.to, pranjulrathour, ~Sept 2026](https://dev.to/pranjulrathour/the-hackathon-demo-that-works-live-a-technical-checklist-4k1)
- "Write the 90-second demo script before writing any code"; two hours on the script "saves six hours of building features that don't appear in the final demo"; by the two-minute mark judges should already have seen the key feature. — [Reskilll idea-to-app](https://blogs.reskilll.com/from-idea-to-app-a-step-by-step-guide-to-hackathon-success/); [Reskilll 3-minute pitch](https://reskilll.com/blogs/hackathon-demo-presentation-tips-pitch-3-minutes-win-2026/)
- Scope test: "if your MVP can't be demoed in under 90 seconds, it's probably too big for a 24–48 hour hackathon." — [WhereUElevate](https://whereuelevate.com/blogs/why-hackathon-projects-fail)
- Smart India Hackathon winner retro (36 hours): judges praised problem fit, a live demo using real data, and robustness, not code quality or stack; regrets were staying awake all 36 hours and making careless bugs by hour 30; rehearsed script five times. — [Reskilll SIH winner](https://blogs.reskilll.com/how-i-won-smart-india-hackathon-lessons-36-hours-changed-everything/)
- 6-hour hackathon winners (dev.to, ~April 2026): their LLM API died mid-event; they survived because they had already routed through OpenRouter and could switch providers; lesson: "prepare two API providers before the hackathon starts," keep a test script exercising both, and set hard spend caps ($5–10) on keys. — [dev.to, md8_habibullah, ~Apr 2026](https://dev.to/md8_habibullah/we-had-6-hours-a-dying-api-and-im-ran-rm-rf-we-still-won-gdf)
- Test the happy path first so the demo flow works before edge cases. — [Reskilll idea-to-app](https://blogs.reskilll.com/from-idea-to-app-a-step-by-step-guide-to-hackathon-success/)
- Paul Graham (2006, still cited by YC's Demo Day guide): "plan for the connection to fail mid-talk and run a local copy of the server." — [paulgraham.com](https://paulgraham.com/investors.html)

### Inferences (12-hour schedule, 9:00–21:00, assuming demos ~19:30–21:00)
Derived by scaling the cited 24-hour plans (freeze at ~10%, skeleton at ~25%, feature-stop at 75–85%) to a 12-hour clock. No source gives a 12-hour plan directly.
- 09:00–10:15 (H0–1.25): lock the one-sentence identity and the single "this is now possible" moment; write the 90-second demo script and the exact click path first; pick stack you already know; choose which speedrun portfolio product you'll integrate for a Best Use prize; set API spend caps and a second LLM provider via a router.
- 10:15–12:00 (H1.25–3): skeleton: one real input → one real output end-to-end, deployed or running locally, committed. This is the "25% mark" target.
- 12:00–16:00 (H3–7): build the wow moment and the demo path only. Seed realistic data. Nothing that isn't on the click path.
- 16:00–17:30 (H7–8.5): integration, happy-path hardening, mock/cache slow external calls, warm AI calls.
- 17:30 (H8.5): feature freeze (two hours before judging per the Sept 2026 checklist). Record the backup video now, on two devices + USB.
- 17:30–19:30 (H8.5–10.5): rehearse 5–10 full runs with a timer; write the one-liner Q&A answers (differentiation, business model, what you'd do with funding/more time, one deep technical question); rotate so at least two people can drive the demo.
- 19:30–21:00: demos. Presenter talks, a second person drives, third watches the clock/handles fallback.

### Gaps
- No first-person retro from a 12-hour specifically (closest: 6-hour and 24/36-hour accounts). The hour-by-hour above is an inference, not a documented winner's schedule.

## Key Question 3: Investor-judged hackathons: weight of business potential vs. technical impressiveness vs. polish; explicit rubrics

### Takeaway
No public rubric exists for SpeedHacks, a16z speedrun hackathons, or YC/Pear/Sequoia hackathons. The best proxy is what a16z speedrun's own investors say they screen for: traction/velocity signal first ("something people want" + "the team that will get it in front of them fastest"), then team (founder-market fit, proof of shipping), then TAM, with a strong "demo over deck" bias and a stated distaste for over-building. Generic investor-style hackathon rubrics score completion, feasibility/business potential, use of tools, and technical excellence each 1–5.

### Cited Findings
- a16z speedrun (SR005 applications review): the three main criteria are traction, team, and TAM, "and traction matters most." Troy Kirwin: "We want signal that this is something that people want and that this is the team that will be able to get it in front of them fastest." Inflated traction numbers are a red flag. — [speedrun: SR005 apps reviewed](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/sr005-apps-reviewed-the-3-signals-that-get-startups-in)
- a16z speedrun ("What We Look for In Applications," SR006 review): "Velocity and traction are not exactly the same thing. Velocity is: How fast are they developing, releasing, and learning?" Early conversations with customers or design partners count as signal even pre-product. Emily Bennett warns against leading customer calls with a solution. They want a concrete plan for the next few months and next hires; put strong news at the top; Marcus Segal says a deck should tell the story in ~12–15 slides. — [speedrun: What we look for](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/what-we-look-for-in-applications)
- speedrun FAQ: a prototype is enough to apply; "No, you don't need a deck. In fact it can sometimes get in the way." Troy Kirwin: "in this AI era, velocity is everything"; teams should "have something out there getting feedback." — [speedrun: top questions](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/top-71-questions-about-a16z-speedrun); [speedrun: Your pitch deck isn't enough, ~Mar 2026](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/your-pitch-deck-isnt-enough)
- speedrun SR007 wrap: "evidence that you know how to ship" makes applications stand out. — [speedrun: SR007 wrapped](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/sr007-apps-wrapped)
- Josh Lu (speedrun GM): a Harvey Ball comparison of competitors isn't a real competitive analysis. — [via Ellenox guide](https://www.ellenox.com/post/how-to-get-accepted-to-a16z-speedrun)
- Third-party guides on speedrun: "demo-first... rewards runnable ideas with evidence of velocity, not narrative"; the intro video is "a communication test, not a production test" (they are not marking down production quality; avoid AI voice generation); "put your strongest thing in the first two minutes, not the last 25." — [Flowjam, 2026](https://www.flowjam.com/blog/a16z-speedrun); [Stackmatix, 2026](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/a16z-speedrun-guide); [annayal.com SR008 guide](https://annayal.com/blog/how-to-get-into-a16z-speedrun/)
- Guillermo Rauch at a speedrun fireside (Mar 2026): "many founders are likely over-building because they can simply prompt a tool to produce results"; choosing problems worth solving is the real differentiator; defensibility now comes from a team's ability to steer models and build verification loops. — [speedrun: Rauch 5 lessons, Mar 2026](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/guillermo-rauchs-5-lessons-for-founders); [speedrun: Rauch unlimited IQ](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/guillermo-rauch-what-will-you-do)
- AI Tinkerers (investor/industry-judged, 2025) rubric: completion; feasibility and long-term business potential; use of tools; technical excellence; each judged independently, max 5 points per category. — [AI Tinkerers Barcelona 2025](https://barcelona.aitinkerers.org/hackathons/h_bC5QnI6_8-c/judging)
- Corporate/investor-style framework: problem importance, feasibility, business impact, novelty, and "certainty of demand (is there evidence people will adopt this?)." — [Ainna corporate hackathon guide](https://ainna.ai/resources/faq/corporate-hackathon-guide)
- "A heavy weight on 'business viability' wants a deck as much as a demo"; "judges can usually tell what's mocked and they penalise it." — [Ainna winning guide](https://ainna.ai/resources/faq/winning-hackathon-guide)
- A 2026 Devpost rubric example: innovation ~20%, technical implementation 25%, problem-solving impact 25%, presentation the rest. — [Masai School, 2026](https://www.masaischool.com/blog/how-to-win-an-ai-hackathon-in-2026-full-guide/)
- Scorejudge: technical execution often outweighs presentation in point allocation; Hacktribe: weights should follow the event's goal. — [Scorejudge](https://scorejudge.com/judging-software-for-hackathons/); [Hacktribe](https://hacktribe.co/blog/fair-balanced-the-hackathon-judging-rubric-blueprint)

### Inferences
- For a prize that is an investor interview, judges will mentally run the speedrun screen: would I take a meeting? That means the pitch must carry evidence of demand (even one real user quote, a design-partner conversation, or the judge's own reaction during the demo) and evidence of velocity (what you shipped in 12 hours, and what's next).
- Technical impressiveness matters only as proof the team can ship fast; polish matters only to the point of readability. "Over-built" reads negatively with this crowd (Rauch, kurbaitaev).

### Gaps
- No explicit YC, Pear, or Sequoia Arc hackathon rubric was found.
- No post-event analysis of past speedrun hackathon winners was found (this appears to be the first "SpeedHacks").

## Key Question 4: Common ways strong teams lose

### Takeaway
The failure modes judges name most are: no working end-to-end demo; over-scoping (which reads as poor judgment); a demo that depends on a live third-party call that fails or a free-tier quota that ran out; deploying a fix in the final minutes; too many slides / showing code or terminals; an unclear one-liner; and a Q&A where the team can't explain AI-written core logic.

### Cited Findings
- "A team that built a platform in 36 hours reads as a team that did not make good decisions." — [dev.to, kurbaitaev, ~July 2026](https://dev.to/kurbaitaev/what-judges-actually-score-notes-from-a-year-of-hackathon-judging-3p4l)
- "The teams that lose points are usually the ones deploying a fix at minute fifty-nine." — [dev.to, pranjulrathour, ~Sept 2026](https://dev.to/pranjulrathour/the-hackathon-demo-that-works-live-a-technical-checklist-4k1)
- Teams that "let AI write their differentiated code then spend the demo Q&A unable to explain how it works." Winners hand-write the differentiating logic because judges ask about it. — [Masai School, 2026](https://www.masaischool.com/blog/how-to-win-an-ai-hackathon-in-2026-full-guide/); [Ainna winning guide](https://ainna.ai/resources/faq/winning-hackathon-guide)
- Free-tier credits exhausted during final presentations (two teams at the Kenya AI Challenge): "on presentation day, the services they depended on had exhausted their free credits." — [dev.to, michaelochieng0](https://dev.to/michaelochieng0/what-building-digicow-at-the-kenya-ai-challenge-taught-me-beyond-writing-code-3hom)
- Vibe-coded build without an API contract: "With mock data, everything looked flawless. But when moving to real data, things started to fracture"; records silently dropped; test vs. production webhooks mixed up. — [LinkedIn, rafal-kiryluk](https://de.linkedin.com/in/rafal-kiryluk)
- Illustrative (unsourced methodology) failure breakdown from a hackathon vendor: poor problem understanding 32%, overambitious scope 27%, weak execution/bugs 21%, bad storytelling 14%, teamwork/planning 6%. Feature-stacking (AI + blockchain + IoT) "usually reads as unfinished." — [WhereUElevate](https://whereuelevate.com/blogs/why-hackathon-projects-fail)
- Too many slides distracts; 6–10 slides, one idea each; judges see many presentations in succession so attention for complex explanations is low. — [SlideModel](https://slidemodel.com/hackathon-presentation/); [Slidestack](https://slidestack.com/blog/how-to-make-a-presentation-for-a-hackathon-easy-guide)
- Don't show code or a terminal; don't go over time. — [AngelHack demo tips](https://angelhack.com/blog/10-tips-to-help-you-rock-your-next-hackathon-demo/)
- Videos inside a live presentation "eat up presentation time because of switching delays." — [HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=6419839)
- "Whoever made the slickest demo wins, regardless of whether their agent actually worked" is a known judging failure; organizers are moving to judge agents on task completion, tool-use accuracy, cost, latency. — [AngelHack AI agent hackathon playbook, 2026](https://angelhack.com/blog/ai-agent-hackathon/)
- "Judges can usually tell what's mocked and they penalise it." — [Ainna winning guide](https://ainna.ai/resources/faq/winning-hackathon-guide)
- Avi Press: be direct about what works and what doesn't. — [JetBrains blog, June 2026](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)
- Venue network was checked "in the general network, but had forgotten to check it in the speaker network." — [ITNEXT tech talk fails](https://itnext.io/tech-talk-fails-we-failed-with-our-live-demos-but-get-the-greatest-feedback-ever-a3f8b860a448)

### Inferences
- Mocking is a tension: judges penalize what looks faked, but judges also advise mocking slow parts. Resolution used by the sources: mock only slow or flaky non-core calls, keep the core "this is now possible" step real, and say explicitly what is mocked.
- "Solving a problem judges don't believe exists" maps to the #1 and #2 ranked criteria (problem the judge feels; named person/situation). With founder-judges, choose a bottleneck they've personally experienced running a startup.

### Gaps
- No quantitative, methodologically sound data on failure-mode frequency was found; the 27%/32% figures are vendor estimates.

## Key Question 5: Demo techniques that reliably land (wow moment, live vs. recorded, handling failure)

### Takeaway
What's documented: show the working thing inside 90 seconds; build the demo around one payoff; let a judge supply one input live; pre-seed realistic data; run locally on a hotspot; keep a labeled recording as fallback and never debug on stage; when something breaks, name the problem in one sentence and show the workaround. I found no sourced evidence specifically for human-vs-agent races or visible timers; that remains an inference.

### Cited Findings
- Show something working within ~90 seconds (Jono Bacon); one "this is now possible" moment beats a feature tour (Bonnie Xu); mock slow parts and pre-fill data (Colin Lowenberg). — [JetBrains blog, June 2026](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)
- Live-demo checklist (~Sept 2026): seed realistic data so the first screen isn't empty; one rehearsed path plus one alternate path; run locally with a phone hotspot (venue Wi-Fi "is the most common single point of failure"); mock or cache slow external APIs and warm up AI calls so cold starts don't eat pitch time; enlarge fonts, zoom the browser, close tabs and notifications; keep a recording and "label it as a recording if you use it"; "let the judge try one input of their choice," which moves the demo from prepared input to live input; when something breaks, "name the problem in one sentence and show the workaround," since judges score composure and understanding. — [dev.to, pranjulrathour, ~Sept 2026](https://dev.to/pranjulrathour/the-hackathon-demo-that-works-live-a-technical-checklist-4k1)
- 3-minute pitch structure: ~20-second problem hook, one-line "what it does," live demo as the longest block (one version: problem 0:00–0:30, solution 0:30–0:40, demo 0:40–2:30, close), stop talking when done; one person presents while a different person drives; memorized script; realistic test data; demo on the actual target device. — [Reskilll 3-minute pitch, 2026](https://reskilll.com/blogs/hackathon-demo-presentation-tips-pitch-3-minutes-win-2026/); [Reskilll idea-to-app](https://blogs.reskilll.com/from-idea-to-app-a-step-by-step-guide-to-hackathon-success/)
- Failure protocol: "Stay composed, switch to the recorded demo while fixing the problem, explain the expected behavior with screenshots, and go back to live if it returns." ## Key Question 6: Pitch structure for investor judges (problem, why now, demo, market/wedge, what you'd do with funding)

### Takeaway
Combine the hackathon-judge ordering (one-liner → felt problem → demo early) with the speedrun/YC seed-pitch elements (what you do, why now, wedge not platform, evidence of demand, team/velocity, the ask). Keep slides minimal, big-text, 1 idea each; lead with the strongest point; have the ask and "next 90 days" ready for Q&A even if not in the pitch.

### Cited Findings
- Paul Graham: "say what you're doing as soon as possible, preferably in the first sentence"; the only thing worth covering before the demo is the problem, "and don't spend more than a tenth of your time on that"; "specific numbers are good"; one person talks; be narrow rather than vague; his biggest worry is "they build something no one wants," so talk about specific users' problems. — [paulgraham.com](https://paulgraham.com/investors.html)
- YC Demo Day guide (2016): the best presentations are clear, exciting, informative, and told as a story. — [YC blog](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/)
- Kevin Hale (YC Startup School, 2019): make it legible, simple, obvious; lead with what you do; start with the problem, not the technology; talk conversationally ("like you're explaining to your mom"); drop jargon, buzzwords, MBA-speak; the audience must grasp customer, product, problem quickly. — [Startup School video](https://startupschool.org/videos/77); [summary](https://allcancode.com/blog/pitch-your-startup)
- YC/Seibel seed pitch elements: what you do, team, traction, unique insights, market size, ask; the company should be explainable in two simple sentences with a specific example; lead with your strongest point rather than standard deck order; "visually boring slides with clear, large text"; investors who talk more are more likely to invest. The ask should state amount, milestones it buys, timeframe (typically 18–24 months). — [SaaStr/Seibel](https://www.saastr.com/the-y-combinator-guide-to-perfectly-pitching-your-seed-stage-startup-with-michael-seibel); [Venture Crew YC framework](https://theventurecrew.substack.com/p/y-combinator-pitch-deck-framework)
- YC (2015): figure out the 5–7 most important ideas people should remember. — [YC blog: better pitch deck](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- Sequoia-style deck (secondhand): "Why Now" is its own section, placed after Problem and Solution and before market size; explain the market/technological/regulatory shift that makes this the moment; don't bury it or merge it with market. — [SlideGenius](https://www.slidegenius.com/?p=102211); [Linas Substack](https://linas.substack.com/p/pitchdeckframework)
- a16z speedrun on wedge (Jon Lai): avoid a "maximum viable product"; start with a narrow wedge the current team can execute well ("right-size problem for the right-size team"); the first wedge often isn't the final one, so learning speed matters; the vision section should explain how owning the wedge earns the right to win the category; a narrow first product a small team can ship in 1–3 months. Nicolas Kopp: a credible wedge that leads into a bigger product is easier to build, sell, and support than a wide platform launch. — [speedrun: How to find your wedge](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/how-to-find-your-wedge); [speedrun: Nicolas Kopp](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/nicolas-kopp-take-the-wedge-every)
- a16z speedrun: investors want a concrete plan for the next few months and next hires; put strong news at the top. — [speedrun: What we look for](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/what-we-look-for-in-applications)
- speedrun 2-minute pitch practices from founders: hook → meat (must match the hook) → payoff; or four one-sentence pillars (problem, solution, founders, traction); identify the 1–3 things that most spike interest; write a full script, practice until near-recitation, film yourself; one founder studied prior Demo Day pitches and broke down their structure first. Investors shout feedback from the front row at Progress Day. — [speedrun: pitch in under 2 min](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/how-to-pitch-in-under-2-min); [speedrun: SR005 Demo Day](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/58-founders-2-minutes-to-pitch-scenes)
- Troy Kirwin: write a short investment memo (3 pages at pre-seed) and generate the teaser deck from it; the memo is the FAQ and shows quality of thinking. — [speedrun: Your pitch deck isn't enough, ~Mar 2026](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/your-pitch-deck-isnt-enough)
- Past YC founders: build the narrative first, metrics support it (Collin); find an angle investors relate to (Rusenko); list the questions you most hope they won't ask and prepare evidence (Orloff). — [LTSE](https://ltse.com/insights/demo-day-tips-from-past-yc-founders)
- Hackathon-specific Q&A to prep: how it differs from existing options, business model, what you'd do with more time. — [Reskilll 2026 guide](https://reskilll.com/blogs/how-to-win-a-hackathon-in-2026-reskillls-guide-from-5000-events/)
- Conflict on problem time: Graham says ≤10% of time on the problem; Masai says ~30% problem / 70% solution; Ainna says spend more demo time on the problem. JetBrains judges stress the problem heavily but want the demo within 90 seconds. — [paulgraham.com](https://paulgraham.com/investors.html); [Masai School](https://www.masaischool.com/blog/how-to-win-an-ai-hackathon-in-2026-full-guide/); [Ainna](https://ainna.ai/resources/faq/winning-hackathon-guide); [JetBrains](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)

### Inferences (suggested 3-minute investor-judge pitch)
- 0:00–0:10 one sentence: who + what + the time it gives back. 0:10–0:35 the felt problem: a named person, the bottleneck, what goes wrong today (one specific number). 0:35–0:45 why now: what became possible this year (Bonnie Xu's "couldn't have built before" framing). 0:45–2:15 live demo: one input (ideally judge-chosen) → wow moment → result; label anything mocked. 2:15–2:40 wedge + market: who pays first, how the wedge expands, one evidence-of-demand data point from today (user quote, sign-ups, design partner). 2:40–3:00 team velocity + ask: what you shipped in 12 hours, what the next 90 days and first hires look like, and what the interview/funding would unlock. Stop.
- Keep ≤6 big-text slides; put the demo on screen for most of the time.

- No a16z or speedrun source on "what would you do with funding" specifically for a hackathon context; the "concrete plan for next few months and next hires" is from their application review.

## Key Question 7: Pre-build vs. build on site; risk management (API outages, Wi-Fi, auth, quotas)

### Takeaway
Pre-build everything boring and risky (auth, deploy pipeline, provider failover, seed data, redirect URLs, trial-account verifications, spend caps) and build the differentiating logic on site. The documented single points of failure are venue Wi-Fi, OAuth redirect allow-lists, free-tier quotas, a single LLM provider, and last-minute deploys.

### Cited Findings
- Hackathon rules vary on pre-work; one 2026 starter-kit post claims teams starting from scratch lose 6+ hours (unsourced) and recommends Next.js + Supabase (auth/Postgres) + Claude/OpenAI + Vercel. Check event rules on prior code. — [Reskilll starter kit 2026](https://blogs.reskilll.com/?p=398)
- Use AI agents for boilerplate and UI scaffolding; hand-write the differentiating logic because judges probe it. — [Masai School, 2026](https://www.masaischool.com/blog/how-to-win-an-ai-hackathon-in-2026-full-guide/)
- Two LLM providers pre-wired (via OpenRouter in the winning case), a test script that exercises both, and hard spend caps on keys set before the event. — [dev.to, md8_habibullah, ~Apr 2026](https://dev.to/md8_habibullah/we-had-6-hours-a-dying-api-and-im-ran-rm-rf-we-still-won-gdf)
- Check spend limits and quotas before the event; two teams ran out of free credits during finals. — [dev.to, michaelochieng0](https://dev.to/michaelochieng0/what-building-digicow-at-the-kenya-ai-challenge-taught-me-beyond-writing-code-3hom)
- Run locally with hotspot fallback; venue Wi-Fi is the most common single point of failure; warm AI calls; cache slow APIs. — [dev.to, pranjulrathour checklist](https://dev.to/pranjulrathour/the-hackathon-demo-that-works-live-a-technical-checklist-4k1)
- Supabase + Vercel auth gotchas: Supabase only accepts OAuth redirects on its Redirect URLs allow-list, and Vercel preview URLs change per deploy, so preview sign-in fails by design unless you add a wildcard or set the site URL per preview; localhost needs a double-asterisk wildcard entry; a preview sign-in can land on the production URL unless `NEXT_PUBLIC_SITE_URL` is restricted to production; server components/route handlers/middleware have no browser session so RLS returns empty results ("Your query runs anonymous, RLS says no"); cookie flags differ on HTTPS. Pre-demo checklist: Supabase Site URL + Redirect URLs, Google Cloud authorized redirect URIs, production-only site URL env var. — [GitHub issue, meal-agent #59](https://github.com/common-origin/meal-agent/issues/59); [Supabase discussion #32687](https://github.com/orgs/supabase/discussions/32687); [Vercel community thread](https://community.vercel.com/t/google-oauth-redirect-url-with-vercel-preview-urls-supabase/6345); [dev.to, toritic](https://dev.to/toritic/supabase-login-works-locally-but-the-session-dies-after-you-deploy-to-vercel-here-is-why-1p2d)
- Twilio trial accounts: calls/SMS only to and from verified numbers (up to 5 verified recipients); verification by SMS only; restricted to sign-up country; custom message bodies not available during trial (templates only); some TwiML verbs blocked; max 4 concurrent calls; trial expires 30 days after sign-up. Upgrade (add payment) before the event to remove these. — [Twilio trial docs](https://static0.twilio.com/docs/usage/trials.md); [Twilio support](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/360036052753); [Twilio error 32100](https://live.docs.dev.twilio.com/docs/api/errors/32100.md)
- Agentic coding pitfalls: agents can pass tests while silently breaking an integration contract; Cursor's agent mode can over-eagerly modify unintended files; Claude Code Pro tier has a hard usage ceiling that fights "walk away for two hours" workflows; plan mode (read-only exploration first) is recommended; hooks can run linters before each commit. — [Codersera 2026](https://codersera.com/blog/ai-coding-agents-complete-guide-2026/); [Futureproofing.dev 2026](https://www.futureproofing.dev/resources/ai-native-team/claude-code-vs-cursor-for-ai-agents-2026); [OpenHands 2026](https://www.openhands.dev/blog/claude-code-best-practices-agentic-coding)
- A hackathon team lost a folder to an `rm -rf` mid-event; coding agents running destructive shell commands is a documented hazard. — [dev.to, md8_habibullah](https://dev.to/md8_habibullah/we-had-6-hours-a-dying-api-and-im-ran-rm-rf-we-still-won-gdf); [Docker blog](https://www.docker.com/blog/coding-agent-horror-stories-the-rm-rf-incident/)

## Key Question 8: How the 3-person role split typically works

### Takeaway
The documented pattern is builder (core feature/demo path, usually backend or the AI piece), interface person (frontend, flow, seeded data), and storyteller (problem statement, pitch, slides, Q&A plan). In a trio, the builder or interface person also owns integration, the repo, and demo rehearsals; the pitch role is the one most often under-weighted and most often decisive. At demo time: one presents, one drives, and at least two must be able to run the demo.

### Cited Findings
- Roles framework: a "builder who owns the core feature and demo path, usually backend or the AI piece"; an "interface person who owns the front end, the flow, and the seeded data"; a "storyteller" who owns the problem statement, the pitch, the slides and the Q&A plan; with only three, "the builder or interface person takes on integration, meaning keeping the pieces working together, managing the repo, and running demo rehearsals"; "a team with only coders and no presenter often loses to a simpler project that's explained better." — [dev.to, pranjulrathour: forming a team](https://dev.to/pranjulrathour/forming-a-hackathon-team-roles-skills-and-the-mistake-most-teams-make-2479)
- Alternative split: backend specialist, designer/UX, and a wildcard covering AI/DevOps/whatever the theme needs; "everyone does everything, but clear ownership prevents dropped balls." — [Medium, Bizthon](https://medium.com/@BizthonOfficial/team-up-and-win-finding-the-right-hackathon-teammates-55c038de4711); [Ainna winning guide](https://ainna.ai/resources/faq/winning-hackathon-guide)
- Designer responsibilities in hackathon teams: UI mockups/prototypes, user-flow diagrams, pitch-deck visuals, demo presentation assets. Research on hackathon teams found three prominent roles: developer, designer, business analyst. — [Cambridge Design Society paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-design-society/article/making-space-for-designers-at-hackathons-uncovering-developerdesigner-tensions-in-hackathon-teams/3231D7B813CE21ADB327CD6A4AF56FB7)
- Favor 2–3 people with a dedicated presenter over a larger specialist team. — [Masai School, 2026](https://www.masaischool.com/blog/how-to-win-an-ai-hackathon-in-2026-full-guide/)
- One person presents while a different person operates the demo. — [Reskilll 3-minute pitch](https://reskilll.com/blogs/hackathon-demo-presentation-tips-pitch-3-minutes-win-2026/)
- At least two teammates should be able to run the working version before the presentation. — [dev.to, stavleak-hackathons](https://dev.to/stavleak-hackathons/your-first-hackathon-prepare-well-and-get-your-project-to-a-working-demo-44ao)
- Paul Graham: have one person do the talking. — [paulgraham.com](https://paulgraham.com/investors.html)
- Align the team early on intent to win: "the worst thing that can happen is that some people try very hard to win while others are just in for the experience." — [HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=6419839)
- Sleep: a 36-hour winner regretted staying awake throughout and made careless bugs by hour 30 (less relevant at 12 hours, but argues for breaks). — [Reskilll SIH winner](https://blogs.reskilll.com/how-i-won-smart-india-hackathon-lessons-36-hours-changed-everything/)

### Inferences
- Mapping to the requested split: Person A (frontend/demo) = interface person + demo driver + owns seed data and the click path; Person B (backend/agent) = builder + integration + repo + provider failover; Person C (pitch/data/design) = storyteller + presenter + owns the one-liner, the "why now," evidence-of-demand collection during the day (e.g., talking to 3–5 people at the venue), the backup recording, and the Q&A sheet. Person C should also be the one tracking the clock and calling the freeze.

## Key Question 9: 2026 tooling that speeds a 12-hour build, and known gotchas under time pressure

### Takeaway
Agentic coding (Claude Code/Cursor) is the default in 2026 but creates Q&A risk if the team can't explain generated core logic and operational risk (destructive commands, silent contract breaks, usage ceilings). Supabase/Vercel is fast but OAuth redirect allow-lists and RLS-in-server-contexts are the top demo-breakers. Voice stacks (Vapi/Retell/ElevenLabs) have 1.4–2.3s real-world tail latency and endpointing defaults that need tuning; Twilio trial accounts can't call unverified numbers. Hosted MCP/Composio need pre-connected OAuth and current API-key enforcement. Browser agents (Stagehand/Browser Use) need cached observe steps, pinned models, and explicit waits.

### Cited Findings
- Agentic coding: plan mode first; hooks for lint-before-commit; agents can pass tests yet break contracts; Cursor agent over-edits files; Claude Code Pro has a hard usage ceiling (Max tiers raise but don't remove it); in one test Cursor Composer was ~2x faster but missed an idempotency key while Claude Code caught a webhook-signature bug. — [OpenHands 2026](https://www.openhands.dev/blog/claude-code-best-practices-agentic-coding); [Codersera 2026](https://codersera.com/blog/ai-coding-agents-complete-guide-2026/); [Futureproofing.dev 2026](https://www.futureproofing.dev/resources/ai-native-team/claude-code-vs-cursor-for-ai-agents-2026)
- Supabase/Vercel: redirect allow-list, preview URL churn, localhost wildcard, production-only site URL env var, RLS returns empty in server contexts without a session. — [GitHub meal-agent #59](https://github.com/common-origin/meal-agent/issues/59); [dev.to, toritic](https://dev.to/toritic/supabase-login-works-locally-but-the-session-dies-after-you-deploy-to-vercel-here-is-why-1p2d); [Vercel community](https://community.vercel.com/t/google-oauth-redirect-url-with-vercel-preview-urls-supabase/6345)
- Convex vs. Supabase: Convex tracks query dependencies and pushes updates with no pub/sub, cache invalidation, or WebSocket setup (vendor claim); Supabase realtime needs configuration and manual refetch decisions; Convex is TypeScript-function queries (weak for SQL analytics) and one user hit bandwidth issues on intensive realtime apps; Supabase is the pick for SQL/Postgres extensions; Supabase paid instances don't pause (no cold starts). — [Convex comparison (vendor)](https://stack.convex.dev/comparing-realtime-databases); [Bytebase](https://www.bytebase.com/blog/convex-vs-supabase/); [UI Bakery](https://uibakery.io/blog/convex-vs-supabase)
- Voice latency (independent benchmark of real calls on one script, 2026): median ElevenLabs 1,424 ms, Vapi 1,558 ms, Retell 1,740 ms; p95 1,768 / 2,008 / 2,259 ms; "judge on the tail." Vapi's published target is p50 <500 ms, p95 <800 ms (a target, not a measurement); Retell's own blog claims 580–620 ms for itself and 500–600 ms for Vapi (marketing). Endpointing defaults matter (Vapi shipped 0.4 s / 1.5 s without punctuation, Retell 1000 ms; both now 0.1 s). Latency depends on provider pairing, region, endpointing, and network; degradation reported at higher concurrency; reproduce on your intended phone routes. — [OpenBenchmarks voice latency 2026](https://openbenchmarks.com/voice-agent-latency/voice-agent-latency-comparison); [Telnyx](https://telnyx.com/resources/voice-ai-agents-compared-latency); [Retell blog (vendor)](https://www.retellai.com/blog/vapi-vs-elevenlabs)
- Twilio trial: verified numbers only (both from and to), 5 verified recipients max, SMS-only verification, sign-up country only, template-only message bodies, blocked TwiML verbs, 4 concurrent calls, 30-day expiry. — [Twilio trial docs](https://static0.twilio.com/docs/usage/trials.md); [Twilio support](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/360036052753)
- MCP/Composio: most connected apps use OAuth and if not connected in advance the agent prompts at runtime; from 2026-03-05 Composio rejects MCP requests without a valid `x-api-key` header (401) for new orgs; the old `mcp.composio.dev` endpoint was removed March 2026 so older tutorials point at a dead URL; self-hosted MCP servers needing OAuth app registration "consistently take multiple sessions to get working"; the official MCP Python SDK had an OAuth-credential-theft flaw fixed in 1.30.0/2.2.0 (Sept 2026); Composio had a May 2026 sandbox code-execution incident compromising ~5,242 connections (reported by competitor Nango). Practical: pre-connect every toolkit, fresh API key, current SDK, recorded fallback since runtime OAuth prompts and token expiry are the likeliest on-stage failures. — [Composio changelog](https://docs.composio.dev/docs/changelog); [Composio MCP guide](https://composio.dev/content/mcp-server-step-by-step-guide-to-building-from-scrtch); [The Hacker News, Sept 2026](https://thehackernews.com/2026/09/official-mcp-python-sdk-flaw-can-let.html); [Nango 2026 roundup (competitor)](https://nango.dev/blog/best-mcp-servers-for-agent-api-integrations/)
- Browser agents: Stagehand gives per-step control (easier to debug/reproduce) vs. Browser Use's higher autonomy for multi-step flows; stability tips: commit the observe cache so the first run is deterministic, pin the model version, add Playwright waits between AI steps, mix deterministic code for reliable steps with AI for changing elements; Browserbase session recordings/screenshots help debugging. — [Scrapfly](https://scrapfly.io/blog/stagehand-vs-browser-use); [QA Skills 2026](https://qaskills.sh/blog/stagehand-ai-browser-automation-guide-2026); [Browserbase docs](https://docs.browserbase.com/use-cases/building-automated-tests)
- Vercel/Supabase/Anthropic are sponsors of 2026 Supabase hackathons and ran a "Zero to Agent" build week (Apr 24–May 3, 2026) pushing Supabase for backend/auth/realtime with v0 and Vercel. — [createwith.com Supabase SF event](https://createwith.com/tool/supabase/events/san-francisco); [Vercel community meetup](https://community.vercel.com/meetups/zero-to-agent-peshawar/37836)
- HN: don't learn a new language/framework at a hackathon; use frameworks and templates you know. — [HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=6419839)

### Inferences
- For a voice demo in a loud venue, the real-world ~1.5–2.3 s tail latency means the demo script must tolerate pauses (narrate during them) and the endpointing setting should be tuned on the venue network beforehand; a recorded call is the fallback.
- Anything requiring a live OAuth consent screen on stage should be pre-authorized; show the already-connected state.

## Consolidated one-page checklist (synthesis of the cited findings above)

Before 9 AM / H0–1:
- One-sentence identity written and tested on a stranger ("which one was the scheduling one" test). [kurbaitaev]
- The felt problem: a named person, their bottleneck, one number. [pranjulrathour; JetBrains/Jono Bacon]
- The single "this is now possible" moment chosen; 90-second demo script and click path written before code. [JetBrains/Bonnie Xu; Reskilll]
- Stack you already know; sponsor product chosen for a Best Use prize. [JetBrains; SpeedHacks listing]
- Two LLM providers behind a router; spend caps; auth redirect URLs allow-listed; Twilio/voice accounts upgraded and numbers verified; hotspot tested in the demo room. [habibullah; Supabase threads; Twilio docs; pranjulrathour]

H1–3: one input → one output end-to-end, committed and runnable locally. [Masai; HN "quarter of the time"]

H3–8.5: only features on the click path; seed realistic data; hand-write the differentiating logic; mock/cache slow non-core calls and say so. [Masai; Lowenberg; Ainna]
